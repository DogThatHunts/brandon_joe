II. The original definition of “citizen” - At Both the State Level and Federal Level

For the purposes of this section we need to review three areas: what constituted citizenship in each of the original 13 States of the Union, what constituted the definition of citizenship at the Federal level, as well as what citizenship looked like at the level of naturalization (meaning: people born elsewhere and coming here to become citizens). Keep in mind as we go through this section, there was no such thing as a “citizen of the United States” yet and that term, as used in the US Constitution, only referred to citizens of each State. I’ll show you all the evidence of that later in this Treatise.

Section A - State Citizenship by Way of the Original 13 States

Birth was the only concern of the States in terms of citizenship because the only other avenue to citizenship, naturalization, was given the to the Federal government under Article I, Section 8, Clause 4. The main focus on this section is to focus in on race, because that is where most of American law quickly went after we became a country. Also, keep in mind that the word “free” is used for both whites and blacks, typically. That word was typically meant to mean, simply: “not enslaved.” It should be noted that babies born by slaves were not born free and would not qualify to be citizens. We will talk about how each of these sections operated leading up to the Dred Scott v. Sandford case, which is where the negro lost all Federal recognition of citizenship (which we will cover later in detail):

Virginia: At the start, the 1779 act drafted by Thomas Jefferson and passed 26 June 1779 declared: “all white persons born within the territory of this commonwealth” citizens.

Massachusetts: The 1780 Constitution (written almost entirely by John Adams, personally) declared that “all men are born free and equal” and contained no racial bar on voting (unlike the rejected 1778 draft, which would have limited voting to white males). Property qualifications applied to men generally. Slavery was held incompatible with the constitution. Free native-born black inhabitants were citizens and, once qualified, were voters from the founding onward. This continued all the way until the Dred Scott case.

New Hampshire: Free native-born inhabitants, including those of African descent, were citizens at the adoption of the State Constitution and the Federal Constitution, and those meeting other qualifications voted. This continued until the Dred Scott case.

New York: The 1777 constitution did not racially limit birth citizenship. Free black men who met the property qualification could vote. Gradual abolition of slavery began in 1799. An 1811 law required black men to obtain a certificate of freedom to vote. The 1821 constitution imposed a $250 freehold (a “freehold” is referring to the value of land possessed that has no debt attached to it. So a “$250 freehold” would require the free blacks to own property valued at at least $250 without any debt attached to it in order to vote) requirement on “men of colour” while lowering barriers for white men. Birth itself was not redefined; free persons born in the state remained citizens, but most free black men lost the practical ability to vote by the Dred Scott case.

New Jersey: Originally, free native-born black inhabitants were citizens, and qualified free black men voted. Early practice therefore treated birth in the state as conferring citizenship on free persons. New Jersey ended black voting in 1807. A substantial free black population remained under unequal rights up until the Dred Scott case.

Pennsylvania: The Declaration of Rights stated that all men are born equally free. Free black men were born as citizens of this State. In 1838 a Constitutional amendment confined the ability to vote to “white freemen,” stripping most free black men of the vote while leaving the underlying birth-citizenship rule for civil status unaltered in statute. Free black people born in the State continued to be treated as inhabitants with limited rights up until the Dred Scott case.

North Carolina: Early constitutions and practice recognized free native-born inhabitants, including free black men who met property tests, as citizens and voters at the founding. Negroes and other persons of color were not allowed to be witnesses against a white man in court. They could only testify in court against another black man. In State v. Manuel, 20 N.C. 144 (1838), Justice William Gaston wrote that slaves who were freed in the State become freemen, “and if born within North Carolina are citizens of North Carolina—and all free persons born within the State are born citizens of the State.” Political power was not required for citizenship. Subsequent statutes imposed heavy disabilities (testimony limits, licensing, restrictions on migration), but the 1838 holding that free birth in the State conferred State citizenship stood up until the Dred Scott case.

Maryland: Birth in the State made free white people citizens in ordinary practice. Free black persons faced registration, testimony, and mobility restrictions that withheld full political citizenship; no general statute equated free black birth to white birth citizenship. Those disabilities remained in force in up until the Dred Scott case.

South Carolina: Pre-Constitution requirement was “born of English parents.” After the Constitution this requirement remained and only free white people born in the State were citizens. Free persons of color were recognized as free but were subjected to extensive legal disabilities and were not treated as full citizens. This continued until the Dred Scott case.

Georgia: The 1777 and later State Constitution treated free white people born in the state as citizens. Slavery was central. Laws progressively restricted free Black residence, required guardians or registration in some periods, and barred political rights. This continued up to the Dred Scott case.

Connecticut: Free persons born in the State were citizens in the ordinary sense. The 1818 Constitution restricted voting to white males. Courts and officials treated free black persons born in the State as holding a limited civil status short of equal political citizenship. This continued until the Dred Scott case.

Rhode Island: Free native-born persons, including free black inhabitants, were citizens; black men voted when they met property tests. The political rights for black men narrowed as the State progressed towards the Dred Scott case.

Delaware: Free white persons born in the state were citizens. Delaware remained a slave state with a large free Black population. Statutes required free Black registration, limited testimony against whites, and restricted migration and assembly.

Section B-1 - State Citizenship as Recognized by the Federal Government

This was entirely prefaced on Article IV, Section 2, Clause 1, which states:

The Citizens of each State shall be entitled to all Privileges and Immunities of Citizens in the several States.

Since there was only two ways to achieve citizenship, birth or naturalization, birth was a State concern because it is domestic in nature, whereas naturalization became the only real concern of the Federal Government.

The only way that we can easily evaluate this is by looking to see if there are any previous examples of blacks entering Federal courts and being recognized as citizens under Article IV, Section 2, Clause 1.

There is really only one that can be found readily which is kind of a unique one because Roger B. Taney (same guy who gave the opinion on the Dread Scott case) was actually opposing counsel on this case: Le Grand v. Darnall, 27 U.S. 664 (1829). Taney actually talks about this case in the Dred Scott opinion.

Besides that case, it was uncommon that a black man would be a party in a Federal case… but it wasn’t unheard of. It occurred occasionally up until the Dred Scott v. Sandford case.

Section B-2 - Federal Naturalization Law for Those Noncitizens Looking to Become Citizens of a State

In the case Boyd v. Nebraska ex rel. Thayer, 143 U.S. 135, 162 (1892), the Chief Justice Melville Fuller gave the definition of “naturalization” to be: “the act of adopting a foreigner, and clothing him with the privileges of a native citizen.”

The power to naturalize was given exclusively to the Federal government in Article I, Section 8, Clause 4, which States: “The Congress shall have Power . . . To establish an uniform Rule of Naturalization.” This makes perfect sense because the entire original purpose of our political system was told perfectly by Thomas Jefferson when he said: “My general plan would be to make the States one as to everything connected with foreign nations and several as to everything purely domestic.” Our original system allows us to have a very individualistic and separate States which operate as separate nations domestically, but then unite together into one on the international sphere.

Section 1 of The Naturalization Act of 1790 is where our journey into naturalization begins, with a text that reads:

Be it enacted by the Senate and House of Representatives of the United States of America in Congress assembled, That any alien, being a free white person, who shall have resided within the limits and under the jurisdiction of the United States for the term of two years, may be admitted to become a citizen thereof, on application to any common law court of record, in any one of the states wherein he shall have resided for the term of one year at least, and making proof to the satisfaction of such court, that he is a person of good character, and taking the oath or affirmation prescribed by law, to support the Constitution of the United States...

For those of you that are new to my research, the term “resident” is quite important in the above quote because it is specifically used in reference to aliens prior to their 2 years being complete (“resident” means “foreigner/immigrant,” which is why people are “residents” during their 2 year period while naturalizing. A “resident” is “someone who is working on becoming a citizen but hasn’t completed that process”).

The term “alien” just meant someone who is not a citizen of a State. We’ll cover that more soon so don’t get too confused about this.

1790 was the first version of the Naturalization Act, but 1802 was the last updated version and the version that is currently active. So what were the initial points that needed to be met in order to gain citizenship in the United States (if not born in a State of the Union)? Well let’s break down the above quote by specific point:

  1. Was previously an alien (not yet a citizen of a State).

  2. Meets the criteria of being a “free white person.”

  3. Has resided (meaning they are not a citizen of a State yet) in the U.S. for 5 years as an immigrant (with one year in the State of application). (it was originally 2 years residence and they extended it to 5 in later updated Acts).

  4. Demonstrates “good moral character” and swears an oath to support the U.S. Constitution.

  5. Relinquishes all other allegiances or citizenships worldwide.

Now, it’s important to keep in mind that there was only “State citizenship” at this time and the term “US citizen” or “United States citizen” was not a specific and separated type of citizenship at this time (and wasn’t until 1866). III. More Quotes to Paint the Path Between Our Beginning and the Dred Scott Case: The Tightening of the Privileges and Immunities Offered to the Negro

In Amy v. Smith, 11 Ky. (1 Litt.) 326 (1822), The Kentucky Court of Appeals rejected the claim that a woman of color was a citizen of Pennsylvania or Virginia entitled to the Privileges and Immunities Clause (I will make personal notes in brackets):

  1. Free negroes and mulattoes [half white/half black] are, almost everywhere, considered and treated as a degraded race of people; insomuch so, that, under the constitution and laws of the United States, they cannot become citizens of the United States. It is true, that when the plaintiff resided in Pennsylvania and removed to Virginia, the constitution of the United States had not been adopted; and prior to its adoption, the several states might make any person they chose, citizens. But as the laws of the United States do not now authorise any but a white person to become a citizen, it marks the national sentiment upon the subject, and creates a presumption that no state had made persons of colour citizens.

  2. And as it respects Virginia, we know that free people of colour have never been considered or treated, either in practice of the country or by the laws of the state, as possessing the rights and privileges of citizens. On the contrary, they are incapable of holding the lowest office in the government; they do not enjoy the right of suffrage, and cannot even be a witness in a civil case. In short, they possess not one of the privileges which are peculiarly characteristic of a citizen, and not even all those which are by courtesy allowed to aliens and strangers, while residing in the state. The plaintiff, then, not being a citizen, either of Pennsylvania or Virginia, cannot be entitled to the benefit of the clause of the constitution in question [referring to Article IV, Section 2, Clause 1]

  3. Prior to the adoption of the constitution of the United States, each state had a right to make citizens of any persons they pleased; but as the federal constitution does not authorise any but white persons to become citizens of the United States, it furnishes a presumption that none other were then citizens of any state; which presumption will stand, until repelled by positive testimony.

In State v. Claiborne, 19 Tenn. (Meigs) 331 (1838), Justice Green, for the Tennessee Supreme Court, upheld a statute making it a felony for a free person of color to enter and remain in the state, against a Privileges and Immunities challenge (notes from me will be in brackets):

  1. Undoubtedly, there may be in a State, a class of people with limited privileges and immunities, who may still be called “citizens,” but it is not in this sense that the word is used in our Constitution. See 1 Niebuhr’s Hist, after 965; Potter’s Greek Ant. book 1, c. 10 — particularly page 73, Boyd’s Ed. Glasgow 1837; Vattel, book 1, sec. 213. The words are: “The citizens of each State shall be entitled to all the privileges.” The citizens meant in this provision are those who are entitled to all, not some privileges and immunities. But free negroes are not entitled to all the privileges of white persons in any of the States. They, therefore, are not citizens in the sense of the Constitution.

  2. Who then is a citizen? Chancellor Kent says, 2 Com. 258, note, that the term is “appropriate to republican freemen;” and certainly among the Romans, where the term had its origin, a citizen was entitled to all privileges, immunities and rights, civil and political. Free negroes have always been a degraded race in the United States, having the right, it is true, of controling their own actions and enjoying the fruit of their own labor, but deprived of almost every other privilege of the free citizen, and constituting an inferior caste in society, with whom public opinion has never permitted the white population to associate on terms of equality, and in relation to whom, the laws have never allowed the enjoyment of equal rights, or the immunities of the free white citizen.

    As this was the description of these people, at the time the Constitution was adopted, can we understand the word “citizen” as used in the section under consideration, as applicable to them?

    “The citizens of each State shall be entitled to all the privileges and immunities of citizens in the several States,” says the Constitution. The citizens here spoken of, are those who are entitled to all the privileges and immunities of citizens. But free negroes, by whatever appellation [definition: a name or title] we call them, were never in any of the States, entitled to all the privileges and immunities of citizens, and consequently were not intended to be included, when this word was used in the Constitution.

    In this country, under the free government created by the Constitution, whose language we are expounding, the humblest white citizen is entitled to all the “privileges and immunities” which the most exalted one enjoys. Hence, in speaking of the rights, which a citizen of one State should enjoy in every other State, as applicable to white men, it is very properly said, that he should be entitled to all the “privileges and immunities” of citizens in such other State. The meaning of the language is, that no privilege enjoyed by, or immunity allowed to, the most favored class of citizens in said State, shall be withheld from a citizen of any other State. How can it be said, that he enjoys all the privileges of citizens, when he is scarcely allowed a single right in common with the mass of the citizens of the State?

    It cannot be; — And, therefore, either the free negro is not a citizen in the sense of the Constitution; or, if a citizen, he is entitled to “all the privileges and immunities” of the most favored class of citizens. But this latter consequence, will be contended for by no one. It must then follow, that they are not citizens.

    But it is contended that they are an inferior order of citizens, subject to many disabilities, and that when they remove into another State, they are entitled to all the “privileges and immunities,” which free negroes in such State enjoy.

    In the first place, this argument is already refuted, if we have shown, that a person entitled to all the privileges and immunities of citizens, cannot be restricted in any rights which any class of citizens exercise.

  3. Upon the whole, by whatever appellation we may designate free negroes, whether as perpetual inhabitants, or citizens of an inferior grade, we feel satisfied, that they are not citizens in the sense of the Constitution and, therefore, when coming among us, are not entitled to all the “privileges and immunities” of citizens of this State.

    It is contended that this act is in derogation [definition: degrading the law] of the 8th section of the bill of rights of this State. We think the word “Freeman” as used in the bill of rights, is of equally extensive signification with the word citizen as used in the Constitution of the U. States: and that although the defendant by his emancipation in Kentucky, obtained a qualified freedom, be did not become a “freeman” in the sense of Magna Charta, or of our Constitution.

    In these instruments, the word is used in its largest sense, and means, as does citizen in the Constitution of the United States, one who is entitled to all the privileges and immunities of the most favored class of the community.

    An emancipated slave, is called a freeman in common parlance, and in reference to his former state, he is so, having acquired privileges and immunities which he did not enjoy before. But in reference to the condition of the white citizen, his condition is still that of a degraded man, aspiring to no equality of rights with white men, and possessing a very few only, of the privileges pertaining to a “freeman” or “citizen.”

    But this provision of our Constitution can only apply to our own citizens, and not to foreigners. If this law applied to Englishmen or Frenchmen it would be constitutional, were it not for treaties and naturalization laws; for surely every free State has a right to prevent foreigners going to it, and to punish those who violate such laws.

    The language must therefore be restricted to freemen of this State, for the protection of whom, alone, the provision is made.

IV. The Dred Scott Case: The Total Re-Defining of the terms “people” and “persons” as Well as the Obliteration of the Federal Recognition of the Negro Under Article IV, Section 2, Clause 1

Originally found in Article 1, Section 9, Clause 1 of our Constitution (The Slave Trade Clause), we see this term “persons” used to describe colored persons of African blood. This distinction between “We the People” and a “persons” is sharply described in Dred Scott v. Sandford, 60 US 393 (U.S. Supreme Court - 1857).

In this case, which is regarding a released slave attempting to gain citizenship of a State. It should be noted here that, since Dred Scott was not born a citizen of his State, his only avenue to citizenship was by way of naturalization… which is exactly why this case went the way it did. So keep that in mind as you read this… this case is essentially a naturalization case but it was so aggressive that it spilled over onto the birth side as well.

Here are the key quotes from the then Chief Justice of the United States Supreme Court, the Honorable Roger Brooke Taney (bold-words and underlines added to bring attention to specific words):

  1. The words “people of the United States” and “citizens” are synonymous terms, and mean the same thing. They both describe the political body who, according to our republican institutions, form the sovereignty, and who hold the power and conduct the Government through their representatives. They are what we familiarly call the "sovereign people," and every citizen is one of this people, and a constituent member of this sovereignty

  2. The question before us is, whether the class of persons described in the plea in abatement compose a portion of this people, and are constituent members of this sovereignty? We think they are not, and that they are not included, and were not intended to be included, under the word "citizens" in the Constitution, and can therefore claim none of the rights and privileges which that instrument provides for and secures to citizens of the United States. On the contrary, they were at that time considered as a subordinate and inferior class of beings, who had been subjugated by the dominant race, and, whether emancipated or not, yet remained subject to their authority, and had no rights or privileges but such as those who held the power and the Government might choose to grant them.

Cornell Law describes it like this:

The incorporation doctrine is a constitutional doctrine through which parts of the first ten amendments of the United States Constitution (known as the Bill of Rights) are made applicable to the states through the Due Process clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.

Prior to the doctrine's (and the Fourteenth Amendment's) existence, the Supreme Court found the Bill of Rights to only apply to the Federal government and to federal court cases. During the signing of the Constitution, every state in the negotiation had different levels of concerns with a too powerful Federal government, and the preamble to the Bill of Rights highlights the importance of the Bill of Rights in limiting overreach by the newly created government. The Supreme Court noted that the Bill of Rights was clearly intended to limit only the federal government (see Barron v City of Baltimore (1833)). States and state courts could choose to adopt similar laws, but were under no obligation to do so.

After the passage of the Fourteenth Amendment, the Supreme Court, through a string of cases, found that the Due Process clause of the Fourteenth amendment included applying parts of the Bill of Rights to States (referred to as incorporation). A lot of contention surrounds whether the Fourteenth Amendment should incorporate any substantive rights, with opinions from Supreme Court justices ranging from complete to no incorporation. Rather than find that the Due Process clause incorporates all of the Bill of Rights, the Supreme Court supported selectively incorporating rights that the Court finds as essential to due process. Under selective incorporation, the Supreme Court incorporated certain parts of certain amendments, rather than incorporating an entire amendment at once.

Some argue that the Privileges or Immunities Clause is a more appropriate textual basis than the due process clause for incorporation of the Bill of Rights but because Slaughter-House Cases dealing with this clause are surrounded by controversy this theory is not supported by the majority of the court.

As a note, the Ninth Amendment and the Tenth Amendment have not been incorporated, and it is unlikely that they ever will be. The text of the Tenth Amendment directly interacts with state law, and the Supreme Court rarely relies upon the Ninth Amendment when deciding cases.
